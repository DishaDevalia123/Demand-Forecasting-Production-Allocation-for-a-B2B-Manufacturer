# Demand Forecasting & Production Allocation for a B2B Garment Manufacturer

I built a system to predict how much a brand-new garment design will sell, before it has ever been shown to a customer, and to turn that prediction into a production quantity. The answer, tested two independent ways, is that **product attributes alone can't do it** — a simple brand × pattern average works as well or better on genuinely new designs. I built the decision layer and the causal analysis anyway, because a negative result only counts if the machinery around it is sound.

Three components: **cold-start quantile forecasting → newsvendor production decision → causal analysis of priority-based rationing.** Synthetic data, generated by a separate process with deliberately injected defects and effect sizes withheld from me.

---

## The business

Modelled on a ~50-year-old garment manufacturer in Mumbai where I was the only data person for two years.

- **Selling happens at booking fairs.** Samples go to ~70 retail shops ("parties") who book by design, colour and size in a two-week window. Then nothing until the next fair.
- **94% of designs at any fair have never existed before.** There is no sales history to forecast from. This is the cold-start problem.
- **Manufacturing takes months**, so production is committed before demand is known.
- **Parties carry a standing priority 1–3.** When production runs short, tier 1 is made first. That rationing rule is what Component 6 investigates.

## Component 1 — Predicting demand for a design that doesn't exist yet
[→ full writeup](docs/component1.md)

Demand is **bimodal**: 24% of designs sell nothing, 60% sell over a hundred pieces, and the average sits where almost nothing lands. So the model predicts the whole distribution — quantile regression, scored on pinball loss — not a single number.

Evaluated with rolling-origin backtesting across five seasons, against brand × pattern empirical quantiles.

**Headline: +18.4% at the 90th percentile. It doesn't survive.** Splitting the test set by whether the design was genuinely new, the model is **−5.7% worse** on the 94% that are, and +51% better on the 6% that are repeats — designs it has effectively seen before. The gain is a small number of large repeat designs dominating a piece-denominated loss.

## Component 2 — Turning a forecast into a production quantity
[→ full writeup](docs/component2.md)

The **newsvendor problem**: stocking out and overstocking cost different amounts, so the optimal quantity is a specific percentile of the demand distribution, `q* = Cu/(Cu+Co)`. This is why Component 1 predicts a curve — an average can't answer it.

One cost was measured rather than assumed: markdown data shows leftover stock realises **68.4% of list price**. The other, gross margin, is unknown, so it's swept across 15–29% rather than guessed.

**Under a per-season capacity ceiling, at 25% margin: fill rate 36.3% → 42.6%, cost ₹906.9M → ₹833.4M.** And the same segmentation applies — on genuinely new designs the model is worse at every margin. Two independent metrics, two cost models, one conclusion.

## Component 6 — Does short-shipping a customer cost you their business?
[→ full writeup](docs/component6.md)

Priority-3 parties get nothing on 35.6% of their order lines, versus 2.1% for priority-1. But priority-1 parties are also the biggest accounts, and big orders get filled better regardless. Untangling that is the work.

Six approaches were rejected on evidence before one estimated: two churn definitions, regression discontinuity (no running variable), classic difference-in-differences (priority never changes), a panel that proved collinear with festival, and a robustness check that tested nothing.

**The rationing is unambiguous** — the tier-1-minus-tier-3 fill-rate gap triples in constrained seasons, with no overlap. **The consequence isn't detectable**: −0.023, CI [−0.153, +0.107], resting on 19 observations. A positive control shows the design detects effects of 10 percentage points or larger, so the null is informative within a stated range. Two placebo tests confirm the method doesn't invent effects.

## What this project actually shows

1. **Product attributes don't predict cold-start demand** in this setting. Established twice, on two metrics, with two model families.
2. **The simple model won three times out of three.** Linear beat gradient boosting on the median. Group averages beat gradient boosting on new designs. Logistic beat gradient boosting on the zero classifier. I kept reaching for flexibility and the data kept saying no.
3. **A decision layer earns its keep.** Pinball loss couldn't choose between two candidate models; realised rupee cost could.
4. **Rationing is visible; its consequence isn't.** Strong first stage, undetectable second stage, with the power calculation to say what "undetectable" means.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic data.** Defects and effect sizes were planted by a separate process and withheld. I don't know whether a causal effect existed to find.
- **Gross margin is assumed** throughout Component 2, and `q*` is hypersensitive to it — hence the sweep.
- **19 observations** carry Component 6's estimate.
- **Something is unexplained.** Production was 1.03–1.06× demand and general-stock share 99.6–99.9% in *every* season, yet fill rate swung 72–96%. Two explanations tested, both failed. Reported as unexplained rather than papered over.
- **Cleaning is not yet reproducible from a script.** Done interactively; the cleaned outputs and decision log survive, the pipeline doesn't.

### Data quality

The generator injected defects without telling me what they were. Full log in [`docs/findings.md`](docs/findings.md). The ones worth naming:

- **Schema drift at a season boundary** — three symptoms, one cause. Priority encoded as text for founding parties and numerically for later ones; design codes using `-` instead of `/`; quantities recorded in dozens instead of pieces. All confined to the same first three seasons.
- **Mixed date formats** — three formats, two mixing day-first and month-first *within the same format*. Resolved by parsing both ways and keeping whichever fell inside the 1–17 day booking window observed on unambiguous rows. Zero unresolved.
- **Orphan keys** — 3,117 rows referencing designs that didn't exist. All recovered, including 157 resolved by matching on the row's own attributes rather than dropping them.
- **Things that looked broken and weren't** — an 18,648-piece order (genuine, largest account), dispatches predating their orders (speculative pre-build), 13 duplicate party names (coincidence — verified by checking they traded concurrently for 8–10 seasons each).
